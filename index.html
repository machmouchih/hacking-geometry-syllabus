01

Course Overview

This studio is about exploring architectural shapes. You will start with a single geometric concept and, through a series of hands-on experiments, evolve it into a complex architectural design. We discover the rules of a shape by hand, and then we encode those rules so the computer can run them for us.

That last step is what makes this course different. A rule you can write down is a rule you can program. By the midterm, you will not just have a model of your shape. You will have a working tool that produces a whole family of them, written by you, with AI as your coding partner. No prior programming experience is expected or required.

Our main reference is Architectural Geometry by Pottmann et al., supported by the class Geometry Almanac.

You will learn to

  • 01 Explore geometric ideas through physical models.
  • 02 Translate those discoveries into precise digital models in Rhino 8 and Maya.
  • 03 Encode your rules into a parametric tool you build yourself.
  • 04 Evolve a simple form into a complex architectural structure.
  • 05 Present your work with high quality renderings and clear diagrams.

02

How This Class Works

This is an online studio, and studios only work when people can see each other's work in progress. Four small rituals keep the class alive between Wednesdays. None of them take long, and all of them count.

📌

The Sunday Pin-Up

Every Sunday by 9pm, post one image of what you are working on and one sentence about what is stuck. That is the whole assignment. Ugly work in progress is not just allowed, it is the point.

Thirty seconds of effort. Counts toward participation.

🔁

Your Response Partner

Each week you are assigned one classmate by name. You reply to their pin-up by Tuesday night. One useful observation, one question. Rotates weekly so you see everyone's project by December.

Nobody posts into silence in this class.

🤝

Your Crit Buddy

One fixed partner for the whole semester. They are the person who notices when you go quiet, the person you text at 11pm when Rhino crashes, and your first reviewer before anything goes to a crit.

Assigned in Week 1.

📓

Your Prompt Log

Keep the conversations you have with AI while building your tool. The dead ends, the rewrites, the moment you realized you were asking the wrong question. This is your process sketchbook, and it is a graded part of both reviews.

Submitted at midterm and final.

💥

The Beautiful Failures Wall

A running gallery of everything that broke. Parameters pushed past the point of sense, geometry that exploded, off by one errors that produced something better than the thing you were aiming for. Post them. At the final review we vote on the best one, and more than one project this semester will end up being built out of a bug.

04

Your Project

One project, running the whole semester, in five phases. Each phase feeds the next. You keep the same concept from August to December, and you watch it change.

1

Experiments

Hands-on form finding. Before you touch a computer, you discover how your geometry behaves by making it with your hands. The class splits into three groups for the opening workshop. You leave with a rule card: your geometry's behavior, written in plain English. That card becomes your code later.

Group tasks and materials ▾

Group A · The Developable Surface

Task: Folding, creasing, and ribboning.

Materials: Cardboard, scoring tools, cutting mats, metal rulers.

Group B · The Tensile Surface

Task: Soap film studies.

Materials: Piano wire, wire in various gauges, pliers, soap solution with dish soap and glycerine, dipping container.

Group C · The Solid Volume

Task: Carving, casting, or modeling with clay.

Materials: Plaster of Paris blocks, modeling clay, carving tools such as rasps, files, and sandpaper.

2

Concept & Taxonomy

Research your assigned concept: its history, its architecture, and the geometry underneath it. Instead of a slide deck that gets shown once and forgotten, you publish an Almanac page that becomes permanent class reference material. Your research must end with a parameter list: what varies in your geometry, and what stays fixed. That list is the specification for the tool you build in Phase 3.

The ten concepts ▾
  • Minimal Surfaces · Frei Otto, Munich Olympic Stadium
  • Developable Surfaces · Frank Gehry, Disney Concert Hall
  • Platonic Solids & Geodesic Spheres · Buckminster Fuller, Biosphere at Expo 67
  • Translational Surfaces & Vaults · Eero Saarinen, TWA Flight Center
  • Ruled & HP Surfaces · Félix Candela, Los Manantiales
  • Freeform Curves & NURBS · Zaha Hadid, Heydar Aliyev Center
  • Freeform & Subdivision Surfaces · Peter Cook, Kunsthaus Graz
  • Pipe Surfaces & Knots · Piano & Rogers, Centre Pompidou
  • Space-Filling Polyhedra · Jean Renaudie, Ivry-sur-Seine
  • Constant Mean Curvature Surfaces · Grimshaw, Eden Project
3

Build Your Own Tool

New this semester

You wrote your rule down in Phase 1. Now you make the computer run it. Using AI as a coding partner, you build a small script that generates your geometry from a handful of inputs. Then you turn the inputs into dials, and suddenly you are not making one shape. You are making every shape your rule allows.

We work in Rhino 8's built-in Python editor, so there is nothing new to install and your scripts operate directly on the models you are already building. You do not need to have written code before. You need to be able to describe what you want clearly, which is a design skill you already have.

The rule of the road

You must be able to explain your own tool. Three sentences per function, in your own words, at both reviews. Code you cannot explain does not count, no matter how well it runs.

4

Evolve

Build detailed models in Rhino 8 and Autodesk Maya, then push the system somewhere new by applying your assigned Exploration Type. Think of this as a second function added to the tool you already built, not a fresh start.

Exploration types ▾
  • Apply striation and pattern
  • Deconstruct into a skeleton
  • Introduce layering and stratification
  • Carve sub-volumes
  • Aggregate as clustered parts
  • Define the boundary condition
  • Develop spliced or interlocked joints
  • Incorporate pleating
  • Differentiate the intensity
  • Create a nested loop
5

Visualize & Present

Document the whole journey and present it. High quality renderings from D5, Twinmotion, Rhino Render, or Midjourney, diagrams that explain your geometry's properties, and a clear account of the architectural implications. Your prompt log and your tool are part of the submission, not an appendix to it.

Weekly Schedule

Wednesdays, August 26 to December 2, 2026. Eleven live sessions plus two peer-led studio weeks. Two Wednesdays fall on BAC closures, so read the calendar carefully and note the gaps in November.

Part 1 · Discover

Aug 26 to Sep 30

Aug 26

Session 1

Launch

Introduction to Hacking Geometry. Concepts and group tasks assigned, crit buddies paired. We finish with a live build: a working parametric script written from scratch in front of you, bugs included. Everyone leaves having run one.

Sep 2

Peer-led

Physical Experiments Workshop (no live session)

Groups run this one themselves. Fold, dip, and carve your way into your geometry. You do not need an instructor watching you make things, you need time and materials. Due Sunday Sep 6: photos of at least three experiments, plus your rule card written in plain English.

Sep 9

Session 2

Concept & Taxonomy

Experiment debrief, then research presentations on your concept's history and geometric principles. Almanac pages assigned. Every presentation ends with your parameter list.

Sep 16

Session 3

Digital Translation & Good Topology

Moving from physical to digital, with a hard look at what makes a mesh clean and why bad topology will haunt you for the rest of the semester. First scripting lab in the second half.

Sep 23

Session 4

Surface Analysis & Rationalization

Reading surface quality through curvature: synclastic and anticlastic, and why the difference decides how a thing gets built. Rhino's Curvature Graph, and an introduction to rationalization for fabrication. Almanac pages due.

Sep 30

Session 5

Build Your Own Tool

From one instance to a whole family. We take your single working script and give it dials. Second half is an open debugging clinic: bring what is broken.

Oct 7

Session 6

Midterm Review

Present your concept, your experiments, and your digital models. Then the part that matters: demo your tool live, and let a classmate work the dials. You cannot fake a working tool, which is exactly why we do it this way.

Submit: rule card, Almanac page, models, working tool, prompt log.

Part 2 · Evolve

Oct 14 to Nov 4

Oct 14

Session 7

Advanced Operations

Exploration Types assigned. How to add a second operation to an existing tool without breaking the first one. We also set up next week's swap and pair everyone off.

Oct 21

Peer-led

Tool Swap (no live session)

The best week of the semester, and it runs entirely without an instructor. You hand your tool to a classmate and they run it on their geometry, while you do the same with theirs. Writing something another person can actually use is a design problem you will never meet working alone. Due Sunday Oct 25: one image made with your partner's tool, and honest notes on where it fought you.

Oct 28

Session 8

Swap Debrief & Tool Refinement

What broke when someone else drove? We fix it, and fold the Exploration Type into your tool. Optional from here: add an interactive demo to your Almanac page.

Nov 4

Session 9

Desk Crits

Individual reviews focused on the spatial and architectural qualities of your evolved form.

Course withdrawal deadline is Friday Nov 6. If you have concerns about your standing, raise them today.
